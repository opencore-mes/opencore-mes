Low-code MES · small plants to 300 mm fabs · AI-assisted

The MES your engineers design, your teams can ask, and your auditors can follow.

OpenCore MES is a manufacturing execution system for advanced manufacturing, from a small plant to a 300 mm fab: semiconductor and electronics plants, medical devices, aerospace and automotive suppliers, where every lot is traced, machines report what they do, and auditors follow every change. Your process and quality engineers design its objects, rules, screens and transactions themselves, with an AI that drafts what they describe, and every change is reviewed and approved by people before it reaches the plant. Once it is running, anyone allowed to can ask an AI what is happening on the floor and get a report, or what a line should process next to meet its shift goal, drawn only from what they may read.

AI Report

Ask the plant. Get a report.

Ask in plain words what is happening on the floor, and an analytics copilot answers with a page: a few sentences, figures, charts and tables, drawn from the plant's live records. It reads; it never changes anything.

In the demo: Ask for a report

  • With your own rightsThe copilot queries as the person who asked, through read-only views that carry their access policies. It sees no row and no field their forms would not show, and every query it runs is in the audit trail.
  • Kept and shared, without sharing dataA kept report holds its queries, not their results. Opened, it is drawn again for whoever opens it, from what they may read.
  • Layouts your engineers designA report layout sets the blocks, their order and their width, and says what each is for. It is reviewed and approved like any design; whoever asks picks one, and the copilot must fill exactly that.
  • AI assisted linesPick the equipment of a line and write its goal for the shift. The copilot looks at what waits and what is done, and says which lots to process first, and why. For the next line, copy the layout and pick its equipment.
  • Asked again, or by the clockKeep what you ask as a prompt. Ask it again as it is or changed, or have it asked every morning, as you and with your rights then; each report it draws is kept as yours.
  • The same report, every morningPin a prompt to a report you like: each run draws the same queries, charts and tables from that morning's data, and the copilot only writes the sentences again, or keeps them as they were.
  • A report becomes a layoutTurn a good report into a report layout, its blocks in their order and width, as a draft your engineers review and approve like any design.
  • Filed, and summed upFile reports under tags of your own and filter by them. Pick several and ask the copilot to sum them up.
  • Advice, never an actionIt moves no lot and changes no priority. Acting on what it says is a person's transaction, with every check.
  • Twenty kinds of chart, drawn from the data aloneBars and lines, areas, scatter and bubbles, pies and donuts, heat maps, box plots and histograms, Pareto, waterfall, sankey, gauges, tree maps, candlesticks and more, with targets and limits drawn across them. The copilot chooses the kind and the columns; every number comes from its query, run for whoever looks. The same charts go on the screens your engineers design, in light and dark, on a phone or a wall board.
  • Show it a photo, a spec, a spreadsheetAttach a picture of a defect, a PDF of the spec or an Excel sheet of limits, or paste a screenshot, and ask how the line compares. The copilot reads them beside the plant's data, and a report can show the photo itself. A kept prompt keeps its files for every scheduled run.
  • Names, never idsEvery record in a report, a chart or the copilot's words is shown by what people call it, M-101, never by an internal id, and only if the reader may see it.

Business rules

The object holds its rules.

A business rule is a short JavaScript function, kept in the object's design beside its fields and states. The screens, the server and every service run that same rule: written once, in one place.

In the demo: A lot, its states, roles and rules

  • One place to lookEngineers open the object and every rule that guards it is there, versioned and approved with it. Nothing to hunt for in screens, services or a separate rules engine.
  • Warned in the UIThe form runs the object's rules as people type and shows the problem beside the field, before anything is sent.
  • Enforced on the server, for services tooEvery save runs the same rules again, whatever sent it: a screen, a transaction, a service, an import. Nothing gets around them.
  • Tested before approvalEach rule carries its test cases, and a draft shows how many existing records it would refuse, before anyone signs.

Flow templates

Routes, plans and input flows, drawn and approved.

How a lot goes through the line, what happens when a reading falls out of its limits, how a broken tool is repaired and when it is maintained, are drawn on one canvas: the Flow designer, zoomed out to see a whole back-end route or in to read one step. A flow template is a design like any other, checked as it is drawn, reviewed and approved before it runs. The core knows nodes, wires and a context; the routes and the plans are yours.

In the demo: The back-end route An out-of-control plan

Routes

Entering a sequence marks where the lot is and its state, and offers the transactions done there; others are refused. Move-in, move-out or any write that moves the lot's step moves it on the route, and the audit trail shows how.

Plans

An event sets one off: a reading out of limits, carrying the lot, the tool and where the lot was on its route; a tool going down, again each time; or a date arriving, such as a preventive maintenance. Its decisions and input screens wait in the bell of the people who must act; photos and files stay with the run. A plan can run another as a sub flow, such as an investigation that several out-of-control plans share, and take its answer back.

Proved before approval

Each node can run a script as it is entered or left. Scenarios walk a lot through a route, or play a plan out from a reading, on copies of real records in a sandbox; a plan that sets off by itself cannot be submitted for review without them.

Input flows: no mouse at the station

Every form already works from the keyboard and the scanner: Enter from input to input, Enter to check and to confirm, and back to the first input for the next lot. An input flow, drawn on the same canvas, says the rest: which input next, what moves on from it (Enter, Tab, a key such as F2, or by itself once a label is read whole), a question only for a lot on hold, the cursor kept on an input in error, confirmed at once, and again. One input flow serves every transaction and screen that names it, across a station's tabs.

UI guides

Panels that explain themselves.

A ? at the top left of a panel opens its guide, docked where the navigation is. It says what each thing on the panel is and how it is used, and pointing at an item outlines it on the page.

  • Written for the designerThe designer, the Flow designer, screens and the sandbox each have their guide.
  • Drawn from the designA form's guide is made from its own design: its fields, what each one takes and which are required. A new field is in the guide the moment it is live.

One path for every change.

A new field, a rule, a screen, a department's approvers: they all go the same way, and the platform keeps the record of it.

In the demo: Changes waiting for approval

  1. Design

    An engineer drafts it, from scratch or from a copy, alone, with co-designers or with the AI, and says why. Work that spans several designs, a field on two objects and the screen that shows it, goes as one change. The platform checks it as they type and tests its rules.

  2. Review

    Someone who did not design it reviews the draft, now frozen, with the evidence beside it.

  3. Approval

    Each department whose part it touches signs once, in its own steps, however many designs the change holds. Never the people who designed or reviewed it.

  4. Execution

    The platform makes it live, exactly as approved, all of it or none of it, on every server and open screen at once: no deploy, no restart.

Version control

Every version kept. Roll back in one step.

Each design in the plant, an object, a screen, a transaction, a route, has a history of versions, and each version came from a change with its reason, its reviewers and its approvers. Nothing is overwritten and nothing is deleted.

In the demo: The lot's design and its versions

  • See exactly what changesA change shows the published version beside the proposed one, element by element, before anyone signs.
  • Test together before approvalChanges still in design go into a shared test sandbox, where they see each other and people try them with their own roles. Nothing there reaches the plant.
  • In a set orderChanges under test are applied one after another, each checked against the ones before it, as it will be when it goes live.
  • Tested with, on the recordEach change keeps what it was tested with, for its reviewers and approvers.
  • Drafted for youThe platform drafts the change that puts things back as they were. It is tried in a sandbox first; if it would break something made since, it says what.
  • One approval to undoA rollback restores what was already reviewed and approved, so one approver, never its author, puts it live. The rollback is a change on the record too.

No restarts. Scales out.

A design change reaches the plant while it runs, and a busy plant gets more servers, not a bigger one.

Changed while it runs

  • An approved change is live the moment it executes: new fields, rules, screens and services, with no deploy and no maintenance window.
  • Open screens redraw with the new design by themselves; nobody signs out.
  • The database upgrades itself when a new release starts, once, however many servers start together.

Designed to scale horizontally

  • Add servers behind a load balancer. A change bus carries every save to all of them, so every screen stays live wherever it is served from.
  • Read replicas take the reads, with a fence that never lets a screen show data older than what was just saved.
  • Shared state lives in PostgreSQL, not in a server, so any one can stop without losing anything.
  • Lists, searches and screens' numbers run in the database, each person's rights compiled into the query: exact over every record, however many there are.

Built on Juris

A framework made for real-time, data-driven systems.

OpenCore MES runs on Juris, a JavaScript framework designed for systems like it: screens that are data, and pages that stay live as the data changes. It ships inside OpenCore MES, so nothing else needs installing.

  • Layout is dataEvery Juris screen is a tree of plain objects, not a template or compiled code. That is why a screen your engineers design can be stored, versioned and approved like any other part of the design, and drawn at once.
  • Live queriesA page subscribes to the queries it shows. A save names what it touched, only those queries run again, once for every screen that shares them, and only the values that changed are sent.
  • Server first, then liveEach page is rendered whole on the server and can be read before any script runs; then it comes alive in place.
  • No build stepNo bundler, no transpiler, no runtime dependencies: a browser runs the source files themselves, only minified in production.
  • Safe by defaultMarkup that could run code is refused, not cleaned up; internal errors never reach a browser; calls are JSON only.

Made for plants that answer to auditors.

OpenCore MES is designed around the practices advanced manufacturing works by: semiconductors and electronics, medical devices, automotive and aerospace suppliers.

In the demo: Lots and their history People and departments

  • Your sign-in, your people. Single sign-on through your identity provider (OpenID Connect), your directory (LDAP or Active Directory), or passwords of its own. Only the people your organization has added may sign in; wrong passwords lock the account for a while, and every sign-in and refusal is in the audit trail.
  • Passwords that answer to Part 11. Passwords expire and are never reused within their history; a second factor from an authenticator app, with recovery codes, optional or required; a session ends after a while with nothing done in it. An administration page sends password links, resets a second factor, lifts a lock and ends sessions, each in the audit trail.
  • People and departments, approved too. Who works here, who belongs where and who approves what change through the same review and approval, typed in or imported from a spreadsheet. Each person is a record your engineers can add to: a badge, a shift, the processes they are certified on.
  • Approved changes only. Design, review, approval and execution for every change to the system, with segregation of duties enforced by the platform.
  • An append-only audit trail. Every write, every refusal by a rule or a transaction, and every signature, each entry chained to the one before it; the database refuses to change or delete one.
  • Changes to records that need approval. Your design says which edits wait for the people responsible, and the form says so before anything is sent.
  • Signed by two people. A transaction can need a verifier: a second person signs in beside the operator, two at most, and each proves it is them at every submit: their password, or a fresh sign-in at your identity provider. Both signatures, names and meanings are kept in the audit trail.
  • Rules with their evidence. Every rule carries test cases, run before a change can be submitted.
  • Who may do what, at a glance. Each object's roles and policies as one colour-coded grid: every role against every field and action, what is granted, read only, denied or conditional, and which policy says so.
  • "Why can't I?" Anything a person cannot see or do is explained from the same rules that decided it.
  • Traceability. Lots, work orders, machines and deviations, linked, with the history of each.
  • Routes and plans with their evidence. Scenarios run on copies of real records in a sandbox, never on the live database, before a change is submitted.
  • Every step of a plan on the record. Each node, decision and input of an out-of-control plan, who and when, in the audit trail; what was entered, with its photos and files, kept with the run.
Open architecture

Built to be open at every seam.

The suites that extend OpenCore MES, the machines it talks to and the systems around it each plug in through a contract that belongs to the core, which is open source (on GitHub, under the Apache License 2.0): anyone can build on it, and the core never depends on what is built on it. Part of it is here today; the rest is planned, and marked so.

Available nowPlanned

Suites

  • A suite adds pages, services, jobs and its part of a design without changing the core.
  • The community edition runs, and is tested, with no suite installed.
  • Several suites side by side: each names its designs apart, may add fields to the core's objects, and keeps what it relies on from being designed away. Suites that would clash are stopped at start.
  • A suite adds its own kinds of design, transaction steps, screen blocks, kinds of schedule and what a service may ask of it, each reviewed and approved like the core's, and alerts beside the name of the person they are for.
  • Remove a suite and the core runs on: what it added waits, labelled, until it is back.
  • A versioned extension API, checked when a suite starts, with a conformance kit to test a suite against it.

Machines

  • SECS/GEM over HSMS: an equipment node holds one connection per tool, sets up the reports each machine needs when it connects, and keeps what the MES has not taken yet.
  • Each machine described as data: a tool type that screens and transactions read, and an interface that maps one vendor's codes and units onto it, approved like any design.
  • Machine events move records through their states, checked by the same rules and recorded in the same audit trail as a person's save.
  • Commands sent from a transaction or a service, last: if the tool refuses, nothing of the run is kept.
  • GEM300 for 300 mm tools: the MES checks every carrier a tool reads at a load port, and lets it in only if it was sent there, with the slot map it expects (E87). Track in creates and starts the control job and process job for its wafers (E94, E40).
  • Cluster tools: each chamber a machine of its own, with its own state, alarms and time, on its tool's one link; a unit with a link of its own still known as part of its tool.
  • Wafers through the tool (E90): each wafer the tool names becomes a record, with its carrier and slot, where it is and how its processing went.
  • Load ports handed to the AMHS (E84): access mode and reservation from a transaction; a port ready to load or unload asks the plant's material control system for a carrier, and a failed hand-off is kept as the tool's alarm.
  • OPC UA, signed and encrypted, and MQTT beside SECS/GEM: the same tool types, screens and transactions, whatever a machine speaks.
  • Each protocol an adapter built on an open contract of the core, with its specification, a schema and a conformance kit every adapter passes.

Systems

  • Web services your engineers design, called with revocable tokens and described by OpenAPI.
  • A service runs your transactions with every check a person's run has: ERP pushes a lot, and the transaction that starts one runs under the service's identity, through the same rules and audit trail.
  • Connections to ERP, LIMS or a label printer, each limited to the requests its design allows.
  • An API for AI agents, with scopes, and no scope that approves.
  • Each API versioned, with notice before anything it promises changes: every answer says its version, and what is to go first answers with when (Deprecation and Sunset). A plant's own web service cannot break the systems that call it without that notice.
  • Scripts run apart. Every rule and service script runs in a separate, locked-down runner that holds no credentials, and one that runs away is stopped. On Linux the operating system walls it in too, with no network and nothing of the machine to read, and each server reports whether it is.
  • Designs, not forks. A plant's differences are designs, approved like any change; nobody edits the core to get them.
  • AI builds on the contracts. Each contract readable by an AI, which drafts and tests a suite, an adapter or an interface; as everywhere, it never approves.
  • Every suite version kept. Each installation keeps a copy of every suite version it ran, so a record can be traced to the code that wrote it.
